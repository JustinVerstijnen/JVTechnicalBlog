In this page contains my course notes of the ITIL 4 course I took back in 2019. Here I will cover the most important subjects from ITIL 4 Foundation. We start with the basic concepts of services and value, and then continue with the Service Value System, the Service Value Chain, the four dimensions, the guiding principles, continual improvement and the most important management practices.

ITIL gives organizations practical guidance for managing IT services. It is not one fixed process that every organization has to follow in exactly the same way. Instead, it helps you understand how people, processes, technology and suppliers can work together to create value for customers and other stakeholders.

When preparing for the ITIL 4 Foundation exam, always use the official ITIL definitions and way of thinking. The way a company works in practice can be different from what ITIL describes, but the exam expects you to follow the ITIL framework.

## Key concepts of service management

### Value and value co-creation

Value is the perceived benefit, usefulness and importance of something. The word *perceived* matters because value is subjective. A provider cannot determine value in isolation: the provider and consumer create it together through an active service relationship.

This is why ITIL 4 speaks about value co-creation rather than simply delivering value. The provider makes capabilities, products and services available, while the consumer contributes requirements, resources, feedback, decisions and actions. Both sides influence the eventual outcome.

Organizations can be service providers and service consumers at the same time. A company may consume cloud infrastructure from one provider, combine it with its own people and applications, and then offer a managed service to another customer.

### Services, products and service offerings

A service is a means of enabling value co-creation by facilitating outcomes that customers want to achieve, without requiring the customer to manage specific costs and risks.

A product is a configuration of an organization's resources designed to offer potential value to a consumer. These resources can include people, knowledge, technology, suppliers, processes and infrastructure. A customer normally sees only the relevant part of a product, not every internal component or delivery process behind it.

A service offering is a description of one or more services designed for the needs of a target consumer group. It can contain:

- Goods: ownership is transferred to the consumer, who becomes responsible for the item.
- Access to resources: the consumer receives access under agreed conditions, but ownership is not transferred.
- Service actions: the provider performs an activity to address a consumer need.

For example, an online mail service may provide access to applications and storage, user support, backup and restoration actions. The underlying product may consist of applications, servers, networks, cloud services, personnel and third-party services.

### Stakeholder and service consumer roles

A stakeholder is anyone with an interest in the activities, services or outcomes of an organization. Stakeholders can include employees, shareholders, communities, boards of directors, suppliers, strategic partners, government agencies, lenders and trade unions.

Within ITIL, we have three important service consumer roles:

- Customer: defines the requirements for a service and takes responsibility for the outcomes of service consumption.
- User: uses the service.
- Sponsor: authorizes the budget for service consumption.

One person can fulfil more than one role. In a smaller organization, the customer may also be the primary user and sponsor.

### Outputs and outcomes

An output is a tangible or intangible deliverable produced by an activity. An outcome is a result for a stakeholder enabled by one or more outputs.

For example, the photographs produced by a wedding photographer are outputs. The ability of the couple and their family to preserve and revisit their memories is an outcome. Outputs matter, but services ultimately exist to support outcomes.

### Costs and risks

A cost is the amount of money spent on an activity or resource. A service can remove costs from the consumer, such as the need to purchase infrastructure or employ specialist staff. It can also introduce costs, including subscription charges, training, network usage and internal administration.

A risk is a possible event that could cause harm or loss, or make it more difficult to achieve objectives. It can also be described as uncertainty of outcome. A provider may reduce risks by managing hardware replacement, specialist availability, security controls or continuity. The service may also introduce new risks that the consumer must understand and manage.

Consumers contribute to risk reduction by clearly defining requirements and outcomes, communicating constraints and critical success factors, and ensuring that the provider can access the resources needed throughout the service relationship.

### Utility and warranty

Utility is the functionality offered by a product or service to meet a particular need. It describes what the service does and determines whether it is fit for purpose. Utility supports the consumer's performance or removes constraints.

Warranty is the assurance that a product or service will meet agreed requirements. It describes how the service performs and determines whether it is fit for use. Warranty typically covers availability, capacity, continuity, security and agreed service levels.

Utility and warranty always have to work together. A service with the correct functionality but inadequate availability does not deliver the expected value. A highly available service that does not support the required outcome is equally ineffective.

## Service relationships

A service relationship is the cooperation between a service provider and a service consumer. It includes service provision, service consumption and service relationship management.

### Service provision

Service provision consists of activities performed by the provider, including:

- Managing provider resources configured to deliver the service
- Providing users with access to resources
- Fulfilling agreed service actions
- Supplying goods where applicable
- Managing service performance
- Continually improving the service

### Service consumption

Service consumption consists of activities performed by the consumer, including:

- Managing the consumer resources needed to use the service
- Using the provider's resources
- Requesting service actions
- Receiving or acquiring goods

### Service relationship management

Service relationship management consists of joint activities performed by the provider and consumer to support continual value co-creation based on agreed and available service offerings. Healthy relationships depend on clear expectations, appropriate communication, feedback and shared responsibility.

## The ITIL Service Value System

The ITIL Service Value System, or SVS, describes how all components and activities of an organization work together as a system to enable value creation. It transforms opportunity and demand into value through five interconnected components:

1. Guiding principles
2. Governance
3. Service Value Chain
4. Practices
5. Continual improvement

These components do not work independently from each other. Governance provides direction and control, the guiding principles support decisions, practices provide organizational capabilities, the Service Value Chain creates operating models and value streams, and continual improvement applies throughout the system.

### Governance

Governance is the means by which an organization is directed and controlled. It establishes direction, evaluates stakeholder needs and organizational conditions, and monitors performance and compliance.

### The Service Value Chain

The Service Value Chain is the central operating model of the SVS. It contains six interconnected activities that transform demand and opportunity into products, services and value:

- Plan
- Improve
- Engage
- Design and transition
- Obtain/build
- Deliver and support

Each activity transforms inputs into outputs. An output from one activity can become an input or trigger for another. Not every value stream follows the activities in the same fixed sequence.

#### Plan

The purpose of plan is to ensure a shared understanding of the organization's vision, current status and improvement direction across all four dimensions and all products and services.

Typical outputs include strategic, tactical and operational plans, portfolio decisions, architectures, policies and improvement opportunities.

#### Improve

The purpose of improve is to ensure continual improvement of products, services and practices across every value chain activity and all four dimensions.

It receives performance data, stakeholder feedback and improvement opportunities. It produces improvement initiatives, plans, progress reports and performance information.

#### Engage

The purpose of engage is to understand stakeholder needs, maintain transparency and support good relationships with stakeholders.

All incoming and outgoing interactions with parties outside the value chain take place through engage. Its outputs include consolidated demand, requirements, feedback, support tasks, contracts, agreements and service performance reports.

#### Design and transition

The purpose of design and transition is to ensure that products and services continually meet stakeholder expectations for quality, cost and time to market.

It translates portfolio decisions and requirements into specifications and prepares new or changed products and services for delivery and support.

#### Obtain/build

The purpose of obtain/build is to ensure that service components are available when and where they are needed and meet agreed specifications.

All new resources enter the value chain through obtain/build, whether they are created internally or acquired from suppliers.

#### Deliver and support

The purpose of deliver and support is to ensure that services are delivered and supported according to agreed specifications and stakeholder expectations.

Its outputs include delivered services, completed support tasks, performance information, improvement opportunities and change requests.

### Value streams

A value stream is a particular combination of value chain activities and practices used to create value for a specific scenario. For example, restoring a failed Wi-Fi service may involve engage, deliver and support, obtain/build and improve, supported by incident management, monitoring, supplier management and the service desk.

Value streams should be designed around outcomes, made visible from end to end and continually improved.

## The seven guiding principles

A guiding principle is a recommendation that guides an organization in all circumstances, regardless of changes in goals, strategies, work or management structure. All seven principles should be considered together.

### Focus on value

Everything the organization does should link directly or indirectly to value for customers and other stakeholders.

To apply this principle:

- Identify the consumers and key stakeholders
- Understand why they use the service and which outcomes they need
- Understand costs, risks and changing expectations
- Consider the complete customer and user experience
- Keep value visible during normal operations and improvement work

Customer experience, or CX, covers the complete set of interactions a customer has with an organization and its products. User experience, or UX, focuses on the user's interaction with a service or product.

### Start where you are

Do not automatically discard existing services, processes, people, tools or knowledge. Assess the current state objectively and reuse what remains valuable.

Direct observation and accurate measurement should be combined. Reports can differ from operational reality, while measurement itself can influence behaviour. Existing success can be replicated or expanded, but sometimes the current state offers nothing suitable for reuse.

### Progress iteratively with feedback

Large initiatives should be divided into smaller, manageable and time-boxed iterations. Each iteration should deliver useful progress, enable learning and reduce the risk of discovering failure too late.

Feedback must be collected and used before, during and after each iteration. Understanding the complete system is important, but analysis should not prevent action. Fast delivery does not mean incomplete or careless delivery.

### Collaborate and promote visibility

Involving the right people improves information, relevance, buy-in and the likelihood of lasting success. Collaboration does not mean that everyone must agree before work can continue.

Work, priorities, bottlenecks, risks and progress should be visible enough to support sound decisions. Communication should be adapted to the audience. Poor visibility can make important improvement work appear unimportant and can conceal work in progress, excess capacity and waste.

### Think and work holistically

No service, practice, process, department or supplier operates in isolation. Organizations should understand how their components work together and how demand is translated into outcomes from end to end.

Changes to one element can affect people, partners, technology, processes and other services. Look for patterns, involve the relevant stakeholders and use automation where it improves integrated visibility and control.

### Keep it simple and practical

Use the minimum number of steps needed to achieve an objective. Remove activities, controls and metrics that do not contribute to value or a necessary outcome.

This does not mean ignoring governance, security or regulatory requirements. A control that appears inefficient locally may still protect the organization as a whole. Simplicity means understanding the purpose of each element and avoiding complexity without value.

### Optimize and automate

Optimization makes work as effective and useful as it reasonably needs to be within constraints such as cost, time, resources and compliance. Automation uses technology or standardized rules to perform steps consistently with limited human intervention.

First understand the context and current state. Then agree the target state, simplify and optimize the work, involve stakeholders, improve iteratively and monitor the outcome. Automating an unclear or inefficient process usually reproduces its problems at greater speed and scale.

## The four dimensions of service management

ITIL uses four dimensions that we have to consider together when designing, managing and improving products and services:

1. Organizations and people
2. Information and technology
3. Partners and suppliers
4. Value streams and processes

### Organizations and people

A formal structure is not enough. An organization also needs an appropriate culture, staffing, competencies, roles, responsibilities, communication and leadership. People should understand how their work contributes to outcomes and value.

### Information and technology

This dimension includes the information, knowledge, applications, infrastructure, communication systems and technical capabilities used to manage and deliver services. Decisions should consider confidentiality, integrity, availability, compliance, usability and technical suitability.

### Partners and suppliers

Few services are produced by one organization alone. This dimension covers relationships, contracts, sourcing strategies, dependencies and the division of responsibilities between the organization and third parties.

### Value streams and processes

This dimension considers how activities are organized and connected to transform demand into value. It includes workflows, controls, procedures, roles, inputs, outputs and opportunities to remove delays, duplication and waste.

### External factors

The four dimensions are influenced by external PESTLE factors:

- Political
- Economic
- Social
- Technological
- Legal
- Environmental

These factors may be outside the direct control of the SVS but can materially affect service design and delivery.

## Continual improvement

Continual improvement is a recurring activity performed at all levels to ensure that organizational performance continues to meet stakeholder expectations. It applies to the complete SVS and to products, services, components, practices and relationships.

The continual improvement model provides seven steps:

1. What is the vision? Connect the initiative to organizational goals and clarify the expected value, stakeholders and responsibilities.
2. Where are we now? Assess the current state and establish a reliable baseline.
3. Where do we want to be? Define the target state and measurable objectives.
4. How do we get there? Create a practical improvement plan.
5. Take action. Execute the planned improvement activities.
6. Did we get there? Evaluate metrics, outcomes and stakeholder feedback.
7. How do we keep the momentum going? Reinforce successful changes, communicate progress and identify the next improvement.

Skipping the vision can cause teams to optimize only their own area instead of the organization as a whole. Skipping the baseline makes it difficult to demonstrate whether the change actually improved the outcome.

## ITIL management practices

A management practice is a set of organizational resources designed to perform work or accomplish an objective. ITIL practices can originate from general management, service management or technical management domains.

### Information security management

The purpose of information security management is to protect the information the organization needs to conduct its business. It manages risks to confidentiality, integrity and availability, as well as authentication and non-repudiation.

### Relationship management

The purpose of relationship management is to establish and nurture links between the organization and its stakeholders at strategic and tactical levels. Relationships should be identified, analysed, monitored and continually improved.

### Supplier management

The purpose of supplier management is to ensure that suppliers and their performance are managed appropriately to support the seamless provision of quality products and services. Collaborative relationships with key suppliers can create value and reduce the risk of failure.

### IT asset management

The purpose of IT asset management is to plan and manage the complete lifecycle of IT assets in order to maximize value, control costs, manage risks, support purchasing, reuse, retirement and disposal decisions, and meet regulatory and contractual requirements.

An IT asset is any financially valuable component that can contribute to the delivery of an IT product or service.

### Monitoring and event management

The purpose of monitoring and event management is to systematically observe services and service components and record and report selected changes of state as events.

An event is a change of state that has significance for the management of a service or configuration item. Monitoring should identify and prioritize events and trigger an appropriate response, including action before a potential fault becomes an incident.

### Release management

The purpose of release management is to make new and changed services and features available for use.

### Service configuration management

The purpose of service configuration management is to ensure that accurate and reliable information about services and their supporting configuration items is available when and where needed.

A configuration item, or CI, is any component that must be managed in order to deliver an IT service.

### Deployment management

The purpose of deployment management is to move new or changed hardware, software, documentation, processes or other components into live, test or staging environments.

Release management makes functionality available for use, while deployment management physically or logically moves components into an environment.

### Change enablement

The purpose of change enablement, called change control in earlier ITIL 4 Foundation material, is to maximize the number of successful service and product changes by assessing risks, authorizing changes and managing the change schedule.

A change is the addition, modification or removal of anything that could have a direct or indirect effect on services.

- Standard change: low-risk, well-understood and pre-authorized.
- Normal change: assessed, authorized and scheduled using the appropriate change authority.
- Emergency change: implemented as soon as possible to resolve an urgent issue, using an accelerated assessment and authorization path.

### Incident management

The purpose of incident management is to minimize the negative impact of incidents by restoring normal service operation as quickly as possible.

An incident is an unplanned interruption to a service or a reduction in service quality. Incidents should be logged, classified, prioritized according to business impact and managed within communicated expectations.

Resolution may come from self-service, the service desk, a specialist support team, a supplier or a temporary major-incident team. In extreme situations, service continuity or disaster recovery plans may be invoked.

### Problem management

The purpose of problem management is to reduce the likelihood and impact of incidents by identifying actual and potential causes and managing workarounds and known errors.

A problem is a cause or potential cause of one or more incidents. A known error is a problem that has been analysed but not resolved.

Incident management restores service. Problem management investigates causes and seeks to prevent recurrence. Problem management includes problem identification, problem control and error control.

### Service request management

The purpose of service request management is to support the agreed quality of a service by handling predefined, user-initiated service requests effectively and in a user-friendly manner.

Requests can include:

- A service delivery action, such as providing a report
- Information or advice
- Provision of a resource or service
- Access to a resource or service
- Feedback, compliments or complaints

### Service desk

The purpose of the service desk is to capture demand for incident resolution and service requests. It is the entry point and single point of contact between the service provider and its users.

The service desk has an important effect on customer experience. It should provide clear channels for issues and requests to be acknowledged, classified, owned and actioned. Channels can include phone, portals, mobile applications, live chat, chatbots, email, walk-in support and messaging platforms.

Automation, AI, robotic process automation and self-service can reduce repetitive low-level work. This allows agents to focus more attention on complex support and high-quality human interactions. Automation should support, not obstruct, the user experience.

### Service level management

The purpose of service level management is to set clear, business-based targets for service levels and ensure that service delivery is assessed, monitored and managed against those targets.

A service level agreement, or SLA, is a documented agreement between a provider and a customer that identifies required services and expected service levels.

Useful SLAs should:

- Refer to a clearly defined service
- Relate to outcomes rather than isolated technical metrics
- Use a balanced bundle of measures
- Reflect a genuine agreement with relevant stakeholders
- Be written clearly and simply

A single metric such as server availability rarely represents the complete service experience. A balanced measurement set can combine availability, transaction success, resolution performance, customer satisfaction and a relevant business outcome.

## Exam-focused distinctions

Below, I have summarized some important differences that are useful to remember for the exam:

| Concept | Main question |
| --- | --- |
| Utility | What does the service do, and is it fit for purpose? |
| Warranty | How does the service perform, and is it fit for use? |
| Output | What was produced by an activity? |
| Outcome | What result was enabled for a stakeholder? |
| Incident | How do we restore normal service quickly? |
| Problem | What caused or could cause incidents? |
| Release management | How do we make a service or feature available for use? |
| Deployment management | How do we move components into an environment? |
| Customer | Who defines requirements and owns outcomes? |
| User | Who uses the service? |
| Sponsor | Who authorizes the budget? |

Also keep the following Service Value Chain rules in mind:

- External interactions are handled through engage.
- New resources come through obtain/build.
- Planning at every level is performed through plan.
- Improvements at every level are initiated and managed through improve.

## Summary

ITIL 4 connects service management to one central idea: organizations and consumers work together to create value.

Products combine organizational resources. Service offerings expose the relevant goods, access and actions to a target consumer group. Service relationships connect providers and consumers, while utility and warranty determine whether the service is both fit for purpose and fit for use.

The Service Value System coordinates guiding principles, governance, the Service Value Chain, practices and continual improvement. The four dimensions prevent organizations from focusing only on tools or processes, and the seven guiding principles provide practical decision support in every situation.

The management practices then turn these concepts into organizational capabilities. Incidents are restored, problems are investigated, changes are assessed, requests are fulfilled, assets and configurations are controlled, suppliers and relationships are managed, and service levels are connected to meaningful outcomes.

The central lesson is that IT service management is not complete when a technical component works. A service must support the desired outcome, provide an appropriate level of assurance, remain understandable to its stakeholders and continually improve as needs and circumstances change.
